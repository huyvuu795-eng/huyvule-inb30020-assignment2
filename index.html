From January, flying out of Singapore costs a little more. Here’s why Singapore Airlines can’t go green alone

Singapore’s world-first green fuel levy shows that cleaner flying depends on partnerships, not one airline’s pledges – and on who captures the value they create.

Book a flight out of Changi Airport today for a departure after 1 January 2027, and your ticket will carry a new line: a sustainable aviation fuel (SAF) levy of between S$1 and S$41.60. Singapore is the first country to charge passengers such a levy. It was due to start in October 2026 but was pushed back after conflict in the Middle East sent jet fuel prices soaring.

Flying accounts for roughly 3.5% of the human-caused warming effect, and about two-thirds of that comes from non-CO₂ effects such as contrails (Lee et al., 2021). SAF – jet fuel made from waste oils and other renewable feedstocks – can cut CO₂ emissions by up to 80%. Yet in 2025 the world produced just 1.9 million tonnes, 0.6% of all jet fuel, at two to five times the price of fossil kerosene (Figure 1).

One airline, many partners

Singapore Airlines (SIA) has committed to at least 5% SAF in its fuel by 2030. It cannot get there alone: its route runs through a Finnish refiner, a regulator, banks and tech firms. That makes SIA a textbook case of the relational view of competitive advantage.

Advantage built between firms

The relational view, developed by Dyer and Singh (1998), argues that partners can earn “relational rents” – gains neither could create alone – from four sources: relation-specific assets, knowledge-sharing routines, complementary resources and effective governance. Singapore’s SAF network shows all four (Figure 2).

How the partnership works

First, assets. Neste’s Singapore refinery, expanded in 2023, can produce up to one million tonnes of SAF a year, which Neste says makes it the world’s largest SAF plant. In 2024, SIA and its budget arm Scoot became the first airlines to receive SAF from that refinery delivered straight into Changi’s fuel hydrant system. Such co-located assets pay off only if the partners stay together.

Second, knowledge. In early 2025 SIA bought another 1,000 tonnes of SAF from Neste, plus the emissions benefits of about 2,000 tonnes from US producer World Energy – without any fuel being shipped – through a “book and claim” system. Each deal teaches partners to source, certify and account for a new fuel.

Airlines bring demand, Neste brings capacity, corporations bring money – and the government brings the rules.

Third and fourth, complementary resources and governance, where the state is the key partner. In October 2025, Singapore’s aviation regulator created SAFCo, a company that collects the levy and buys SAF centrally. In a 2026 trial, SAFCo pooled orders from nine buyers, from SIA and Scoot to Google and two banks, completing its first delivery in August. Airlines bring demand, Neste brings capacity, corporations bring money and emissions targets, and the government brings rules plus a fixed “cost envelope” that gives everyone price certainty. This stretches a theory built for pairs of firms: here the state is a partner pooling demand, not just a referee.

Why partnerships matter for the SDGs

This matters because SAF has long been stuck. Studying airlines, fuel makers and policymakers, Dodd and Yengin (2021) found “free-riding across sectors”: each waited for the others to invest first. Supply-chain interviews point to shared levers that no stakeholder can pull alone (Ahmad & Xu, 2021). By guaranteeing demand through a levy and pooled tenders, Singapore is trying to break that deadlock.

In UN terms, the network serves SDG targets 13.2 (climate policy), 7.2 (renewable energy) and 9.4 (cleaner industry), and SAFCo itself is a working model of target 17.17 on public-private partnerships. Yet the feedstock problem below is an SDG 12 issue: if “waste” oil is really virgin palm oil, Singapore’s gains could be paid for by deforestation elsewhere. Cross-sector partnerships are increasingly judged by whether they shift whole systems, not single organisations (Clarke & Crane, 2018).

The catch: scale and value capture

The numbers are sobering. S&P Global analysts estimated that Singapore needs around 90,000 tonnes of SAF a year to meet its 1% target; SIA’s flagship Neste purchases were 1,000 tonnes each. The 1% goal itself has slipped to 2027, and the 3–5% target for 2030 now depends on global SAF availability.

Partnerships also raise a harder question: who keeps the value? Dyer, Singh and Hesterly (2018) show that value creation and capture shift over an alliance’s life, with bargaining power shaping who gains. In SAF, producers hold the scarce resource. IATA complains that in mandated markets suppliers have widened margins so that airlines pay up to five times the price of conventional jet fuel. SAFCo’s competitive tenders, due from late 2026, are in effect a governance tool to stop a few suppliers capturing most of the rent.

Limits no partnership can fix

Feedstock is the hardest limit. Most new SAF capacity relies on used cooking oil and animal fats. Campaign group Transport & Environment reports that Europe imports 80% of the used cooking oil it burns as fuel, raising fears that virgin palm oil is being relabelled as waste. Becken, Mackey and Lee (2023) estimate that net-zero aviation roadmaps could require 30% of sustainably available biomass by 2050. No partnership can create more waste oil.

No partnership can create more waste oil.

There is also a credibility risk. Peeters and colleagues (2016) show how aviation has repeatedly promoted “technology myths” that postpone harder choices, and SAF does little about non-CO₂ warming. Passengers are wary too: in a UK survey, most travellers liked low-carbon jet fuel but would not pay more for it, with trust a key factor (Xu et al., 2022).

Singapore’s design partly answers this. The levy must appear as a separate line on every ticket, and premium passengers pay four times more than economy passengers (Figure 3) – a fairer split, since they use more space and fuel per seat. In Rawls’s (1971) terms, the burden falls most on those best able to bear it. Yet transit passengers are exempt, protecting Changi’s hub business while letting many long-haul flyers off.

Lessons for Southeast Asian airlines

Research on airline alliances shows that carriers gain most from “constellations” that pool large volumes of traffic (Lazzarini, 2007). In the SAF era, fuel partnerships may become the new alliances. Vietnam Airlines already taps this network: its first SAF flight, in May 2024, refuelled with Neste fuel in Singapore. For Vietnamese and other ASEAN carriers, Singapore offers three lessons: sign long-term deals with several suppliers, demand traceable feedstock, and push governments to pool demand regionally.

The levy on your ticket next year will be small. The real test is whether the partnerships it funds deliver verifiable emission cuts – and share the gains fairly between airlines, suppliers and the people who fly.
